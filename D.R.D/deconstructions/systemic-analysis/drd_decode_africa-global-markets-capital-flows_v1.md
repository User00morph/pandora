## STAGE 3 — EVIDENCE EXTRACTION

### CATEGORY 1 — GOVERNMENT-TO-GOVERNMENT CAPITAL: CHINA, GULF STATES, US/EU | Tier 2-3

**China — the incumbent, resource-for-infrastructure model.**
~$500B invested across Africa since 2000. ~$15B specifically into DRC mining. Built via the 2007 Sicomines resource-for-infrastructure agreement — Chinese SOEs now embedded across mining, processing, logistics, trading infrastructure in DRC. New duty-free market access policy effective May 1, 2026 creates direct incentive for African mineral exports to flow toward Chinese markets over Western-aligned supply chains. China also committed $1.4B to rehabilitating the Tazara railway (Zambia–Tanzania).
**Tier:** 2-3 (think tank / Africa Center reporting, traceable to documented trade agreements).
**Pattern:** Two decades of continuous engagement = structural lock-in, not opportunistic deals. China's model trades infrastructure now for resource-extraction rights long-term, with downstream processing retained at home.

**Gulf states — the rising challenger, equity-stake model.**
Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar increasingly active — the Future Minerals Forum (Riyadh) is becoming a key convening point. UAE signed a $1.9B deal with Sakima (DRC state mining co.) for four critical mineral mines (2023), followed by a $1.1B investment in Zambia's Mopani Copper Mines. Gulf SWFs are direct equity participants, not just lenders.
**Tier:** 2-3 (Atlantic Council / Chatham House — credentialed policy analysis).
**Pattern:** Gulf capital is positioning as a third option between Chinese resource-extraction terms and Western ESG-conditioned capital — faster to deploy, fewer governance strings attached than Western DFI capital.

**US/EU — smaller, slower, conditionality-heavy.**
US Development Finance Corporation + Minerals Security Partnership: $200M+ into African mining projects — an order of magnitude smaller than China or Gulf commitments. The Lobito Corridor (rail link from DRC/Zambia copper belt to Angola's Atlantic coast) is the flagship Western-aligned infrastructure counter-move to China's eastern corridor.
**Tier:** 2 (Brookings, Chatham House).
**Pattern:** Western capital is reactive (countering China) rather than initiating, and comes wrapped in ESG/governance conditions that slow deployment relative to Chinese and Gulf capital.

### CATEGORY 2 — CRITICAL MINERALS: COBALT, LITHIUM, GOLD | Tier 2-3

**Production leadership (2024-2026):** DRC, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mali, Namibia, South Africa, Morocco lead cobalt/copper/gold/PGM production. Zimbabwe leads continental lithium output; advancing a $450M refinery at Mapinga Industrial Park. DRC lithium project — $1.4B investment cited as transforming the sector.

**The decisive pattern — African governments asserting resource nationalism in real time (2025-2026):**
- Zimbabwe: restricted raw lithium exports; considering chrome export limits
- Botswana: mandated 24% of new mining concessions sold to local investors
- Ghana: banned mining in all forest reserves (revoked a 2022 regulation that had allowed it)
- Malawi: temporary ban on all raw mineral exports
- DRC: banned cobalt exports outright
- Mali, Ghana, Zimbabwe: mandating national equity stakes in lithium projects

**Tier:** 2-3 (Brookings, Devex, African Energy Chamber — credentialed + serious independent reporting, cross-confirmed across multiple outlets).
**Pattern — this is the single most important finding in this research pass:** the extraction relationship is shifting from "African resource, foreign capital + foreign terms" toward "African resource, foreign capital + African equity + African export control." This is not yet sovereignty in the full sense (foreign capital and foreign demand still drive the market), but it is a real and measurable shift in who captures the value. Institutional investors are responding by requiring heavier ESG/compliance frameworks (IFC Performance Standards, UN Global Compact) as the price of entry — meaning the capital flowing in now is increasingly the kind that will tolerate African-side conditions, not flee them.

### CATEGORY 3 — AFRICAN SOVEREIGN WEALTH FUNDS | Tier 2

**Scale:** Sub-Saharan Africa: ~$160B AUM in SWFs + ~$244B in public pension funds. 36 African countries now operate SWFs (more than $100B aggregate, deployed increasingly toward both financial return and strategic development).
**New entrant:** Guinea launching its first SWF by Q2 2026, initial target $1B.
**Strategy mix:** 33% strategic mandate, 24% stabilization, 13% savings (remainder mixed). At least a third of African SWFs exist specifically to attract foreign private capital as a co-investment partner on local development — i.e., the SWF's job is to be the credibility anchor that unlocks larger foreign tickets.
**Tier:** 2 (Cleary Gottlieb legal/regulatory analysis, Wilson Center, AfDB — credentialed institutional sources, internally consistent across sources).
**Pattern:** Most African SWFs are under-capitalized relative to the mineral wealth they sit on top of — meaning the leverage point (per Category 2 above) is real but currently small relative to what it could be. This is a structural opportunity gap, not a closed door.

### CATEGORY 4 — FINTECH / CAPITAL RAILS: NIGERIA + KENYA | Tier 2-3

**Kenya overtook Nigeria as Africa's top startup-investment destination in 2025** — $984M raised, +52% YoY, led by climate-tech alongside fintech.
**Nigeria retains fintech-specific dominance** — 32% market share of Africa's fintech sector, $1B+ VC raised 2021-2023.
**Concentration:** Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, Egypt collectively capture 84% of all African startup funding — the rest of the continent (54 countries) splits the remaining 16%.
**Active capital:** Ingressive Capital ($50M Pan-African fund, Egypt/Ghana/Kenya/Morocco/Nigeria, pre-seed/seed focus) is a representative example of the VC layer actively deploying.
**Tier:** 2-3 (Techpoint Africa, Businessday NG, fintech-specific trade press — serious independent reporting, internally consistent).
**Pattern:** Capital concentration in 4 countries means fintech-rail opportunity outside Nigeria/Kenya/SA/Egypt is comparatively starved — relevant if D.S.E wants a less-crowded entry point versus competing directly in the most contested markets.

## STAGE 5 — RECONSTRUCTION

### 1. WHAT IS NOW KNOWN WITH HIGH CONFIDENCE

Africa is not a passive recipient of capital — it is the contested ground of an active three-way competition (China resource-for-infrastructure, Gulf equity-stake capital, Western ESG-conditioned capital), and African governments are using export controls and equity mandates in real time to capture more value from that competition than they have historically. This is happening now, in 2025-2026, not as theory.

Capital concentration is extreme at every layer: 4 countries capture 84% of startup funding; one ETF (AFK) markets pan-African exposure while sitting 40% in a single country; SWF assets are real ($160B+) but small relative to the resource base underneath them.

### 2. WHAT IS PROBABLE BUT NOT ESTABLISHED

The resource-nationalism shift (export bans, equity mandates) will translate into durable African-side value capture, rather than being negotiated away once Chinese/Gulf/Western capital exerts counter-pressure. The trend is clear and recent (2025-2026); whether it holds against capital that can simply go elsewhere (the threat every resource-exporting nation faces) is not yet resolved.

Botswana-style blockchain transparency infrastructure is a viable model for other African mineral economies — the mechanism is proven, the question is adoption rate elsewhere (DRC cobalt, Zimbabwe lithium) where governance incentives to obscure rather than reveal are stronger.

### 3. WHAT REMAINS GENUINELY UNCERTAIN

Whether the under-capitalization of African SWFs relative to mineral wealth represents an open door (foreign/diaspora capital can co-invest at the SWF layer and capture upside) or a closed one (SWFs structurally exist to be the credibility wrapper for the largest institutional players, not individual or smaller sovereign capital). This needs direct investigation of specific SWF co-investment terms before any D.S.E action.

Individual exchange performance (JSE/NSE/GSE) relative to the ETF wrappers — not resolved this pass, flagged below.

### 4. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR D.S.E (capital deployment Layer 2)

- **AFK is not a clean pan-African bet — it is a leveraged South Africa bet with extra fees.** If the thesis is continent-wide diversification, AFK does not deliver it; treat it as SA exposure with a marketing label.
- **The resource-nationalism trend (Category 2) is the actionable signal**, not the ETF layer. Direct or fund-level exposure to companies operating *with* African government equity participation (rather than against it) is structurally better-positioned than legacy extraction-model plays.
- **Fintech-rail opportunity is most open outside the Nigeria/SA/Kenya/Egypt concentration** — the 16% remainder covering 54 countries is comparatively under-capitalized and under-competed.
- **Niger Delta (Urhobo/Ijaw) flag carried forward from memory** — this regional angle has not been cross-referenced against this capital-flow research yet. Recommend a dedicated follow-up pass once the Ancestral Architecture decode is pulled alongside this one.
